# Showcase composition

## Default priority

Balance aesthetics and clarity, with demonstrations primary and paper exposition secondary. This applies to every visual direction, including the editorial option. It does not imply a particular palette, animation style, page length, or fixed media-to-text percentage. Follow a user's explicit preferences when they change this balance.

The visitor's path is: see a meaningful effect → understand what to notice → compare or explore it → optionally investigate how it works.

## Select the material before arranging it

Assign each candidate example a role:

- **Anchor:** a representative, legible demonstration that introduces the central capability. Prefer an example a new visitor can understand quickly.
- **Capability example:** demonstrates a distinct property, task, or condition not already clear from the anchor.
- **Comparison:** reveals a relevant difference under stated conditions.
- **Extended collection:** additional variety for visitors who want to explore further.

Selection is editorial, not an exhaustive asset dump. Avoid many near-identical examples or choosing only spectacular footage unrelated to the paper's contribution. A curated showcase is legitimate; do not present it as a statistical summary. Preserve the context of failures or limitations relevant to any claim made on the page.

For each featured example, finish this sentence in the working notes: "Watch ___ to see ___." If this cannot be completed from the evidence, its role is unclear. Rewrite the viewing cue or choose a more informative example.

## Opening composition

Combine a compact project identity, a plain-language capability sentence, resource links, and a real demonstration. Author names remain readable, but the logo, title, affiliations, and decorations should share space intentionally with the demonstration.

Choose one lead rather than giving every element equal emphasis. A wide teaser, a small coordinated media group, or a useful input/output pair can lead. On smaller screens, place the demo directly after the essential identity/resources and avoid excessive vertical gaps.

The opening should let visitors answer: what does this project do, where is an example, and where can I get the paper/code? Do not require a decorative transition, slow intro animation, or long abstract first. If media is not ready, a meaningful poster and clear play/loading state should preserve the composition.

Judge the mobile opening independently of the desktop one. A two-column desktop hero can become a long stack of title, prose, authors, affiliations and resource buttons before any media appears. Reflow these elements when necessary: keep essential identity and primary resources together, and place a complete readable author block immediately after the demonstration if that better serves the page. Measure and inspect the first useful media's actual position; shrinking all text is not the remedy.

For a comparison-led opening, check which result actually appears first on mobile. A tall baseline card can push the proposed result out of view even when the group begins early. Consider a labeled result-first arrangement or another compact paired view, while keeping reading/focus order coherent. Replace a tall stack of selector buttons with a labeled compact control when it helps the media remain visible; do not hide which example is selected.

## Page rhythm

A useful sequence, adapted to the paper:

1. Identity and anchor demonstration.
2. A few distinct capability showcases with brief viewing cues.
3. An interpretable comparison or interactive exploration.
4. A compact explanation of the mechanism and selected quantitative support.
5. Optional deeper material, resources, and citation.

Alternate a dominant media area with smaller supporting examples or concise explanation when this improves scanning. A uniform grid for every section flattens the hierarchy; a new visual treatment for every section fragments the page. Use a small coherent set of layouts.

One section should have one clear question or capability. Organize galleries around properties visitors can recognize, such as long-term consistency, instruction changes, editing locality, or task diversity, when supported by the paper. A sequence of "Results 1/2/3" headings is less useful.

## Explanation in layers

Keep three levels distinct:

- **Immediately visible:** what this example shows, its task or input, method labels, and conditions essential to interpret a comparison.
- **One interaction away:** full prompts, additional examples, settings, detailed captions, and higher-resolution inspection.
- **Later in the page or linked paper:** full abstract, detailed mechanism, comprehensive experiment tables, derivations, and related work.

Do not hide a caveat that changes the meaning of the visible claim. A long prompt can be collapsed; a different sampling speed between compared methods cannot be buried as incidental metadata.

Method prose should connect an observable effect to the relevant idea. A compact annotated diagram may do more than several paragraphs. Reuse the actual paper evidence, with shorter web-oriented explanation, while preserving its meaning.

## Aesthetics that improve viewing

- Spend visual space on media, legible captions, and meaningful grouping before adding decoration.
- Use one dominant accent and a restrained hierarchy of backgrounds and borders as appropriate to the chosen direction. Do not impose those colors on research plots when it would change their meaning.
- Give important media enough size to inspect. Do not fill a desktop row with so many tiny videos that the claimed difference becomes invisible.
- Preserve aspect ratios and comparison scale. Preview cropping is acceptable only when it does not remove the phenomenon being demonstrated and full inspection remains available; scientific comparison frames should retain relevant content.
- Let controls appear in predictable places. An attractive poster must still make playback discoverable. Hover effects supplement touch/keyboard controls.
- Check the hero's play affordance in the first viewport along with the poster. A large visible video whose only play button sits below the fold still delays the first useful effect; an unobtrusive, keyboard-accessible overlay can solve this without shrinking the media.
- Use motion sparingly outside the demonstrations. Several moving backgrounds, marquees, and autoplay groups compete with the result the visitor is supposed to watch.
- Let density change with device size. Mobile may show fewer previews with explicit expansion instead of shrinking every desktop tile.

## Acceptance questions

Review the rendered page, not just the design description:

- Before reading a long passage, can a visitor find and inspect a representative effect?
- Does each featured section explain what to notice with little reading?
- Is there a clear primary example rather than a wall of equally weighted cards?
- Can comparison differences be understood at the displayed size and playback state?
- Are the prompts and details available without crowding the initial view?
- Do headings, controls, and captions remain legible with paused, playing, and failed media?
- Does decoration frame the effect rather than becoming the main event?

If one fails, repair selection, composition, labeling, or interaction before adding more content or visual effects.
